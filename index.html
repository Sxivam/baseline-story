The realisation

A friend's pre-op bloodwork started this.

Before a minor surgery, his doctor ordered a basic panel. He booked it on a pharmacy app for a few hundred rupees. I didn't even know testing was that cheap — and I care about this more than most.

So I ran it on myself. I'm the fittest in my group: gym daily, clean diet, cardio. I expected a clean sheet. I didn't get one. You can't know what's happening inside you from how you look or how much you lift.

Then I made my whole friend group test.

Ten high performers tested. 10 out of 10 came back with hidden issues — quietly draining their energy, mood and focus.

One friend's vitamin D was barely survivable, while she blamed her consulting job for the exhaustion. In a group built for being driven and health-aware, the deficiency wasn't the exception. It was the default.

The problem

People optimise what they can see and ignore what they can't. Even when something makes them test once, nothing keeps them in the loop — so the issues silently return.

The approach

The product isn't a test. It's the loop the test should trigger.

01
Test
Two ways in. Upload an existing report, or tap the marketplace and book the cheapest panel near you. One step, either way.
02
Baseline AI
AI reads the PDF, maps every marker to its reference range, and writes your baseline in plain English. The feeling first ("the low-energy one"), the clinical name second. In seconds, no typing.
03
Plan
A handful of adaptive questions on what's actually drifting. Then a four-week plan that unlocks one week at a time. Foundations, food, movement, re-test. Two moves a week, checkable on the dashboard.
04
Forecast
A directional estimate, not a model from one data point. Your value, the season, and your diet flag the likely drift window. "D usually slides by winter. Worth a re-test in October."
05
Nudge & re-test loop closes
A quiet weekly check-in lands in your inbox. When the forecast says it's time, the right panel surfaces again. The loop closes and restarts.

The test is the cold start. The loop is the product. It works whether you've tested once, never, or yearly.

The share mechanic

People don't share that they're sick. They share that they're winning.

That's the unlock. A bad result is private; a good one is a flex. So the shareable baseline isn't a medical report — it's a scorecard. When your numbers are strong, you want people to see them. That instinct is the entire distribution engine.

The cadence is school, not the clinic
Half-yearly · the report card
A full-body panel. The complete picture, twice a year — the one you actually post when it's good.
Quarterly · the unit test
A cheap, targeted re-check of only the markers flagged as risky last time. If they're clean, the half-yearly is enough. If not, you caught it early.

Everyone remembers the rhythm of report cards and unit tests. Baseline borrows it — so testing stops feeling clinical and starts feeling like a score you're improving.

the wedge for men
Testosterone is the most competitive number a man can post.

For men, testosterone tracks directly with energy, drive, strength, focus and mood — and population-level testosterone has been falling for decades, generation over generation. Most men have never measured it once. Put a real number on it and two things happen: the ones who score well broadcast it, and the ones who don't finally have a reason to act. Either way, it spreads.

The leaderboard instinct is the growth loop. A scorecard people are proud of travels further than any ad I could buy.

The ranking, and why it compounds

A score is just a number. A percentile is a competition.

Bryan Johnson's whole following exists because he can say he's in the top fraction of a percent for his age. That's the pull. The moment Baseline can tell someone where they actually rank, the scorecard stops being information and becomes status — and status is what travels.

Stage 1 · with the first data
Percentile score
As soon as there's a population to compare against, every marker gets a percentile for your age and sex. "Your vitamin D beats 82% of men your age." Instantly legible, instantly shareable — the Bryan Johnson framing, for everyone.
Stage 2 · as the data deepens
An Elo-style rating
With enough data, a single composite rating — chess-style — that blends your markers into one number that moves as you improve. It rewards the loop: re-test, push the number up, climb the ranks. The score itself becomes the reason to come back.
Stage 3 · the shareable flex
Ranked by region, state, country
Opt-in leaderboards: where you stand in your city, your state, India, the world. People share a top-percentile card the way they share a marathon time. Each share is a billboard pointing back to the test.
Why this is a moat, not a feature
A percentile is only as good as the population behind it. The more people test, the sharper everyone's rank gets — and the harder it is for a latecomer to replicate. The ranking improves with scale, scale comes from sharing, sharing comes from the ranking. The data flywheel and the growth loop are the same loop.
How it makes money

Distribution is free. The money sits in the loop itself.

The mistake would be to lead with ads — low CPMs, and ads in a health product erode the exact trust that makes people share. So I'd layer it: own the relationship first, monetise the testing I'm already driving, then add margin on top once the habit sticks.

Layer 1 · now
Test referral — the cash engine
Every panel booked through the marketplace pays a commission. Labs already pay handsomely for high-intent customers. And because the loop drives re-tests, it's recurring revenue, not one-off — the same user books two to four times a year.
Layer 2 · once the habit sticks
Subscription — the margin
A low monthly tier for people hooked on the loop: trend history, sharper forecasts, family profiles, priority re-test slots. Pure margin on users who've already proven they value the product.
Layer 3 · at scale
Brand placement — the cherry
Only once I own a large, trusting, self-testing audience. Supplement and health brands pay to reach the exact moment a user learns they're low on something — native, relevant, never a banner. A layer, never the foundation.
The moat
Distribution and speed. No one owns the relationship with the young Indian self-tester yet. A creator-led wedge lets me blitzscale that relationship while the category is still fragmented. The re-test loop turns a one-time audience into recurring, defensible revenue before anyone else shows up.

What I learned showing it around

I put v1 in front of five people from my test group. They changed it.

They skipped the forecast
Four of five went straight to the plan and ignored the projection. So I moved the plan above the forecast. People want the next move first, the why second.
"Plain language" wasn't plain enough
"Ferritin" meant nothing to them. Now the baseline leads with the feeling, not the marker. "The low-energy one" before the clinical name.
The marketplace was the real hook
The ones who hadn't tested cared less about the dashboard and more about "just tell me the cheapest test." So booking moved earlier in the flow.

None of these were what I expected to hear. That's the point of putting it in front of people early.
